# UNIT 1: Health & Fitness


## Lesson: Definition of fitness and components of fitness

## What you will learn
- Define fitness.
- Define each of the 11 components of fitness.
- Give a sporting example for each component.

### Definition of fitness
**Fitness** is the ability to meet the demands of your environment and daily life (and your sport) without becoming overly tired, and with energy left over to enjoy other activities. Fitness is not one thing. It is made up of several **components**, and different sports need different mixes.

[Diagram: The 11 components of fitness: five health-related and six skill-related]
### The 11 components
| Component | Definition | Sporting example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cardiovascular endurance** | The ability of the heart and lungs to supply oxygen to working muscles for a long period | Distance running, cycling |
| **Body composition** | The proportion of fat, muscle, bone and water in the body | Lean mass for a gymnast |
| **Flexibility** | The range of movement possible at a joint | A gymnast's splits |
| **Muscular endurance** | The ability of a muscle (or group) to keep contracting repeatedly without tiring | Rowing, many push-ups |
| **Speed** | How quickly you can move your body (or a body part) over a distance | A 100 m sprint |
| **Strength** | The maximum force a muscle can produce in one effort | A one-rep-max lift |
| **Agility** | Changing direction quickly while keeping control | Dodging a defender |
| **Balance** | Keeping the centre of mass over the base of support, still or moving | A gymnast on a beam |
| **Co-ordination** | Using senses and body parts together smoothly and accurately | Hitting a ball with a bat |
| **Reaction time** | The time between a signal and the start of your response | Reacting to the starter's gun |
| **Power** | Force produced quickly (strength × speed) | A standing jump, a sprint start |

Components 1–5 are often grouped as **health-related** (important for everyone's health). Components 6–11 are **skill-related** (they help you perform in sport). Notice that **speed** and **strength** can appear in either group depending on the course, so always check your own handbook.


## Lesson: Fitness testing

## What you will learn
- Describe the protocol for one standardised test for each component of fitness.
- Explain validity, reliability and limitations of fitness tests.

### Why test fitness?
Testing gives a **baseline**, shows **strengths and weaknesses**, helps you **set goals**, and lets you **monitor progress** by retesting. A test must be **standardised**: the same equipment, surface, instructions and conditions every time.

### One test for each component
| Component | Standardised test |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular endurance | Multistage fitness (bleep) test |
| Body composition | Skinfold calipers or body-fat scales (BMI is a rough guide only) |
| Flexibility | Sit-and-reach test |
| Muscular endurance | One-minute push-up test (or curl-ups) |
| Speed | 30 m or 40 m sprint |
| Strength | Grip strength dynamometer |
| Agility | Illinois agility run |
| Balance | Stork stand |
| Co-ordination | Alternate-hand wall toss |
| Reaction time | Ruler drop test |
| Power | Standing long jump (or vertical jump) |

## Lesson: Use of fitness testing data

## What you will learn
- Explain how and why scores are compared with **normative data**.
- Choose and read graphs and tables to analyse fitness and performance.

### Normative data
**Normative data** are typical scores collected from large groups of people of a similar age and sex. Comparing your score with them tells you whether you are **below average, average or above average** for your group.

Why compare?
- To find strengths and weaknesses.
- To set realistic goals.
- To check whether your fitness is in a healthy range.

But remember: norms are *averages*. Everyone starts from a different place, so **improvement against your own baseline** is often more meaningful than comparing with others.

### Tables and graphs
| Use this | When you want to show |
|---|---|
| **Table** | Exact values, and many results at once |
| **Bar chart** | Comparing separate categories (for example your score against the average for each test) |
| **Line graph** | Change over time (for example progress across weeks) |

[Diagram: Bar chart comparing a student's results with the average for their age group]
[Diagram: Line graph of bleep test level across 12 weeks of training]
### How to read a graph
1. Read the **title** and both **axis labels** (with units).
2. Look at the **scale** and the **key**.
3. Describe the pattern or comparison, using numbers.
4. Explain what it means for fitness and what to do next.


## Lesson: Innovative tools for performance

## What you will learn
- Describe how AI tools and wearable technology can track and monitor performance.
- Evaluate the benefits and limitations of these tools.

### Technology in training
Fitness watches, heart-rate straps, GPS trackers, smart insoles and phone apps all use **sensors** to collect data (heart rate, speed, distance, steps, sleep). **AI and software** then look for patterns in the data and turn them into **insights**: training zones, recovery scores, trends and suggestions.

[Diagram: Sensors collect data, software and AI analyse it, and insights are shown to the athlete]
### How they help
- Track progress and trends over time.
- Show heart-rate zones while you train.
- Help spot signs of over-training (rising resting heart rate, poor sleep).
- Personalise training and keep you motivated.
- Allow coaches to monitor a squad.

### Limitations and risks
- **Accuracy varies** with the device, how it is worn, and what is being measured.
- They are **not medical devices**. Do not use them to diagnose a problem.
- **Privacy:** health data can be shared, stored or sold. Check the settings and who can see your data.
- **Too much data** can overwhelm you or cause anxiety. Data should support, not replace, how you feel and your coach's advice.
- Data can be wrong, or even faked.


## Lesson: Maximum oxygen uptake (VO₂ max)

## What you will learn
- Define VO₂ max.
- Explain why it is a measure of cardiovascular endurance.
- Describe how it can be measured or estimated.

### What is VO₂ max?
**VO₂ max** is the **maximum volume of oxygen** (in millilitres) that your working muscles can take in, transport and use **per kilogram of body mass per minute**. It is written in **ml/kg/min**.

A higher VO₂ max means your heart, lungs and blood can deliver oxygen more efficiently, so you can keep going at a higher intensity. That is why it is the standard measure of **cardiovascular endurance**.

[Diagram: Illustrative VO₂ max ranges for untrained teenagers up to elite endurance athletes]
### How is it measured?
- **Laboratory test (gold standard):** you run or cycle with increasing difficulty to exhaustion while wearing a mask that measures the oxygen you breathe in and the carbon dioxide you breathe out.
- **Field tests (estimates):** the **multistage fitness (bleep) test** and the **Cooper 12-minute run** estimate VO₂ max from your score. They are cheaper and easier but less exact.

### What affects VO₂ max?
Genetics, age, sex, training (it can improve with regular aerobic training), body composition, altitude and health.


## Lesson: Factors affecting fitness

## What you will learn
- Describe a range of **physical, psychological and social** factors that affect fitness and performance.
- Explain how a factor can help or limit performance.

[Diagram: Physical, psychological and social factors that affect fitness]
### Physical factors
- **Age and sex:** fitness changes through life. Hormones affect muscle and body fat.
- **Genetics:** body type, muscle fibre types and VO₂ max potential are partly inherited.
- **Diet and hydration:** the right fuel and fluids support training.
- **Sleep and recovery:** the body adapts and repairs while resting.
- **Health, illness and injury:** these can stop or reduce training.
- **Lifestyle:** smoking, alcohol and inactivity reduce fitness.
- **Training and environment:** the type of training, heat, cold and altitude.

### Psychological factors
- **Motivation** to start and keep going.
- **Stress and anxiety** can affect sleep, energy and focus.
- **Confidence and attitude** to exercise and to challenges.
- **Mood and mental health.**

### Social factors
- **Family and friends:** support or pressure to be active.
- **Peers and role models.**
- **Culture and beliefs** about sport and exercise.
- **Access to facilities, equipment, coaching and clubs** (and the cost).
- **Time:** school, work and family commitments.
- **Media and social media** influences.


## Lesson: Principles of training: SPORT and FITT

## What you will learn
- Explain the principles of training (**SPORT**).
- Use **FITT** to plan, progress and adjust training.

### SPORT
| Letter | Principle | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | **Specificity** | Training should match the demands of your sport and the component you want to improve | A swimmer does swimming sets, not only running |
| **P** | **Progression** | Increase the demand gradually so you keep improving and avoid injury | Add a little distance or weight each week |
| **O** | **Overload** | Work harder than you normally do so the body has to adapt | Running further or faster than usual |
| **R** | **Reversibility** | Fitness gains are lost if you stop training | Fitness falls during a long break |
| **T** | **Tedium** | Boredom reduces motivation, so add variety | Mix running, cycling and circuits |

Progression and overload work together as **progressive overload**.

[Diagram: Fitness rises in steps with progressive overload and falls when training stops]
### FITT: how to apply overload
| Letter | Stands for | Ways to change it |
|---|---|---|
| **F** | **Frequency** | How often you train (sessions per week) |
| **I** | **Intensity** | How hard you train (speed, weight, % of max heart rate) |
| **T** | **Time** | How long each session lasts |
| **T** | **Type** | The training method (continuous, interval, circuit, weights) |

Change **one** FITT variable at a time and build up gradually. Allow time for **rest and recovery**.


## Lesson: Essential elements of a general training programme

## What you will learn
- Describe the four essential elements of a general training session.
- Explain the purpose of each element.

[Diagram: A training session: warm-up, endurance training, resistance training, cool-down]
### 1. Warm-up and stretching
1. **Pulse raiser:** light activity such as jogging to raise heart rate and body temperature.
2. **Mobility and dynamic stretching:** leg swings, arm circles, walking lunges.
3. **Sport-specific practice:** drills that copy the movements to come.

**Why:** warmer muscles work better, joints move freely, blood flow increases and the risk of injury is lower. You also get mentally ready.

### 2. Endurance training
Training the **cardiovascular system** with continuous, interval or fartlek work (for example jogging, swimming, cycling). It improves how well the heart, lungs and blood deliver oxygen.

### 3. Resistance training
Training that makes muscles work against a **resistance** (body weight, free weights, machines, bands). It builds **strength** and **muscular endurance** and helps protect joints.

### 4. Cool-down
1. **Gradually reduce activity** (slow jog or walk) so heart rate and breathing come down.
2. **Static stretching:** hold each major muscle stretch for about 15-30 seconds.

**Why:** helps the body return to its resting state, reduces stiffness and is a good time to improve flexibility.


## Lesson: Methods of training

## What you will learn
- Describe six methods of training.
- Match a method to a component of fitness and a sport.

### The six methods
| Method | What it is | Improves | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Continuous** | Steady exercise at the same pace for a long time (at least about 20 minutes) with no rest | Cardiovascular endurance | A 30-minute jog |
| **Fartlek** | "Speed play": the pace and terrain change throughout the session | Cardiovascular endurance and some speed | A run mixing jogging, sprints and hills |
| **Interval** | Periods of hard work alternating with rest or low-intensity recovery | Speed, power and endurance | 8 × 200 m sprints with a 1-minute rest |
| **Circuit** | A series of exercise stations completed one after another | Muscular endurance, strength and general fitness | 10 stations of 30 seconds each |
| **Weight (resistance)** | Lifting body weight, free weights or machines | Strength (heavy loads, few reps) or muscular endurance (light loads, many reps) | Squats, bench press |
| **Plyometric** | Explosive jumping and bounding exercises | Power | Box jumps, depth jumps |

[Diagram: Intensity profiles of continuous, interval and fartlek training]
### Choosing a method
Use the **specificity** principle: match the method to your sport and the component you want to improve. A sprinter might choose interval and plyometric training. A marathon runner might choose continuous and fartlek.


## Lesson: Monitoring of exercise intensity

## What you will learn
- Estimate maximum heart rate and describe heart rate training zones.
- Use the **Karvonen method** to calculate a target heart rate.

### Why monitor intensity?
Training must be hard enough to cause adaptation (overload) but not so hard that it causes injury or over-training. **Heart rate** is an easy, useful guide to how hard your body is working.

### Heart rate training zones
**Estimated maximum heart rate (max HR) = 220 − age.** Training zones are a **percentage of max HR**.

[Diagram: Heart rate training zones for a 15-year-old with an estimated maximum heart rate of 205 bpm]
Zone names and percentages vary between sources, but in general:
- **Low zones (about 50-70%):** recovery and aerobic base.
- **Middle zone (about 70-80%):** aerobic fitness. This is the usual **aerobic training zone**.
- **High zones (about 80-100%):** anaerobic training and maximal effort. Only for short periods.

You can measure your pulse at the **radial** (wrist) or **carotid** (neck) pulse for 15 seconds and multiply by 4, or use a heart-rate monitor.

### The Karvonen method
The Karvonen method also uses your **resting heart rate**, so it personalises the target to the individual.

**Heart rate reserve (HRR) = max HR − resting HR**

**Target HR = (HRR × intensity %) + resting HR**

[Diagram: Worked example of the Karvonen method]


## Lesson: Indicators of over-training

## What you will learn
- Define over-training.
- Describe physiological and psychological indicators.
- Suggest how to prevent or respond to over-training.

### What is over-training?
**Over-training** happens when the training load is too high and recovery is too short, for too long. Instead of getting fitter, performance **stops improving or falls**. Teenagers who are still growing, who specialise in one sport very early, or who feel pressure from others can be at higher risk.

[Diagram: Performance rises with balanced training, then falls when training load is too high]
### Indicators
| Physiological (body) | Psychological (mind) |
|---|---|
| Performance stops improving or drops | Low motivation, loss of enjoyment |
| Constant tiredness and heavy legs | Irritability and mood swings |
| Higher resting heart rate | Anxiety or low mood |
| Slower recovery of heart rate after exercise | Trouble concentrating |
| Frequent illness or infections | Loss of confidence |
| Muscle soreness that does not go away, more injuries | Feeling "burnt out" |
| Poor sleep or loss of appetite | Dreading training |

### Prevention and what to do
- Follow **progression**: increase the load gradually.
- Include **rest days** and quality sleep.
- Eat enough and stay hydrated.
- Vary the type of training (avoids tedium).
- Monitor resting heart rate, sleep and mood.
- If you notice several signs, **reduce the load** and talk to a coach, teacher, parent or doctor.

## What you will learn

### Four functions of the skeleton
| Function | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Shape and support** | Gives the body its shape and holds soft tissue up | The vertebral column holds the body upright |
| **Movement** | Bones act as levers; muscles pull on them across joints | The femur and tibia when you kick |
| **Protection** | Shields delicate organs | The cranium protects the brain; the ribs protect the heart and lungs; vertebrae protect the spinal cord |
| **Blood production** | Red and white blood cells are made in bone marrow | Marrow in the femur, pelvis and sternum |

(Some courses also list **storage of minerals** such as calcium.) An adult has **206 bones**.

### Axial and appendicular skeleton
- **Axial skeleton (green):** the central axis: **cranium, vertebral column, ribs and sternum**.
- **Appendicular skeleton (red):** the limbs and the girdles that attach them: **clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, pelvis, femur, patella, tibia, fibula** and the hands and feet.

[Diagram: Labelled skeleton showing the axial skeleton in green and the appendicular skeleton in red]
### Main bones to know
Cranium, vertebral column, sternum, ribs, clavicle, scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges, pelvis, femur, patella, tibia, fibula, tarsals, metatarsals.

## Lesson: Joints and types of joint

## What you will learn
- Define a joint.
- Describe fixed, slightly moveable and freely moveable (synovial) joints with examples.

### Definition
A **joint** is a place where **two or more bones meet**.

[Diagram: Fixed, slightly moveable and freely moveable joints]
| Joint type | Movement | Structure | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** (fibrous) | None | Bones are joined by tough fibrous tissue | Plates of the cranium |
| **Slightly moveable** (cartilaginous) | Small | Bones are separated by a pad of cartilage | Between the vertebrae |
| **Freely moveable** (synovial) | Wide range | A fluid-filled capsule lets bones glide | Shoulder, elbow, hip, knee, wrist |

### Types of synovial joint
| Type | Movement | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Ball and socket** | All directions plus rotation | Shoulder, hip |
| **Hinge** | Bending and straightening only | Elbow, knee |
| **Pivot** | Rotation | Top of the neck (turning the head) |
| **Condyloid** | Movement in two planes without rotation | Wrist |
| **Gliding** | Small sliding movements | Between carpals and between vertebrae |
| **Saddle** | Back-and-forth and side-to-side | Base of the thumb |


## Lesson: Features of a synovial joint

## What you will learn
- Name the main features of a synovial joint.
- Explain the function of each feature.

[Diagram: Structure of a synovial joint]
| Feature | Function |
|---|---|
| **Articular cartilage** | Smooth, slippery layer covering the ends of the bones. Reduces **friction** and absorbs shock |
| **Synovial membrane** | Lines the inside of the joint capsule and **produces synovial fluid** |
| **Synovial fluid** | Fills the joint cavity. **Lubricates** the joint and nourishes the cartilage |
| **Ligaments** | Tough, slightly elastic bands joining **bone to bone**. Hold the joint together and limit unwanted movement |
| **Joint capsule** | A tough sleeve that surrounds the joint and holds the fluid in |

**Tendons** are not part of the joint, but they cross it: they attach **muscle to bone** so that muscles can move the joint.

## Lesson: Origin and insertion of muscles

## What you will learn
- Define the **origin** and **insertion** of a muscle.
- Locate the origin and insertion of the biceps.
- Explain how origin and insertion produce movement.

### Definitions
Skeletal muscles are attached to bones by **tendons**.
- **Origin:** the attachment of a muscle to a bone that **does not move** (or moves least) when the muscle contracts. It is usually the end closest to the centre of the body.
- **Insertion:** the attachment to the bone that **moves** when the muscle contracts. It is usually further from the centre of the body.

When a muscle **contracts** (gets shorter), it pulls its **insertion towards its origin**.

[Diagram: The biceps: origin on the scapula, insertion on the radius]
### Example: the biceps
- **Origin:** the scapula (shoulder blade).
- **Insertion:** the radius (forearm), by a tendon just below the elbow.
- **Action:** when the biceps contracts, the forearm is pulled up towards the shoulder, so the elbow **flexes**.

## Lesson: Muscle movement: antagonistic pairs

## What you will learn
- Explain why muscles work in antagonistic pairs.
- Identify the agonist and antagonist for movements at the elbow, knee and hip.

### Muscles only pull
Muscles can only **pull** (by contracting). They cannot push. So to move a joint in two directions you need **two muscles working in opposite directions**. This is an **antagonistic pair**.
- **Agonist (prime mover):** the muscle that **contracts** to cause the movement.
- **Antagonist:** the muscle that **relaxes** (lengthens) to allow the movement.

When the movement reverses, the roles swap.

[Diagram: Biceps and triceps as an antagonistic pair at the elbow]
| Joint action | Agonist | Antagonist |
|---|---|---|
| Elbow flexion | Bicep | Tricep |
| Elbow extension | Tricep | Bicep |
| Knee flexion | Hamstrings | Quadriceps |
| Knee extension | Quadriceps | Hamstrings |
| Hip flexion | Hip flexors | Gluteus maximus |
| Hip extension | Gluteus maximus | Hip flexors |
| Ankle plantar flexion | Gastrocnemius | Tibialis anterior |
| Ankle dorsiflexion | Tibialis anterior | Gastrocnemius |


## Lesson: Muscle contraction types

## What you will learn
- Define isometric and isotonic contractions.
- Distinguish concentric and eccentric isotonic contractions.
- Give sporting examples.

[Diagram: Isometric, concentric and eccentric muscle contractions]
| Type | What happens to the muscle | Does the joint move? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Isometric** | Muscle contracts but stays the **same length** | No | Plank, wall sit, holding a handstand, pushing against a wall |
| **Isotonic: concentric** | Muscle **shortens** while contracting | Yes | The upward phase of a bicep curl; driving up from a squat |
| **Isotonic: eccentric** | Muscle **lengthens** while contracting under load | Yes | Lowering the weight in a bicep curl; landing from a jump |

**Isotonic** means the muscle changes length. Concentric and eccentric are the two kinds of isotonic contraction. Eccentric contractions help us **control** a movement and absorb force.

Carbohydrates, proteins and fats are **macronutrients**: we need them in **large amounts**.

| Nutrient | Main functions | Food sources | Energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | The body's main and quickest source of energy. Stored in muscles and liver as **glycogen** | Bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, oats, fruit | About **4 kcal** per gram |
| **Proteins** | **Growth and repair** of muscles and tissues. Also used to make enzymes and hormones | Meat, fish, eggs, dairy, beans, lentils, tofu, nuts | About **4 kcal** per gram |
| **Fats** | **Energy store**, insulation, protecting organs, and absorbing vitamins A, D, E and K | Oils, nuts, seeds, avocado, oily fish, butter | About **9 kcal** per gram |

### Types of carbohydrate and fat
- **Complex carbohydrates** (wholegrains, oats, vegetables) release energy slowly. **Simple carbohydrates / sugars** (sweets, sugary drinks) release energy quickly.
- **Unsaturated fats** (olive oil, nuts, avocado, oily fish) are healthier choices. **Saturated fats** (fatty meat, butter, pastries) are best eaten in smaller amounts.

[Diagram: Vitamins, minerals, fibre and water: jobs and food sources]
### Vitamins and minerals (micronutrients)
We need these in **small amounts**, but they are essential for health.

| Nutrient | Why we need it | Food sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Calcium** | Strong bones and teeth, muscle contraction | Milk, cheese, yoghurt, fortified plant milks, leafy greens |

## Lesson: Energy requirements and expenditure

## What you will learn
- Explain **energy balance**.
- Describe **BMR** and the factors that affect energy requirement.
- Read food labels to compare foods.

### Energy balance
Energy is measured in **kilocalories (kcal)** or **kilojoules (kJ)** (1 kcal ≈ 4.2 kJ).
- **Energy intake (in):** the energy from the food and drink you consume.
- **Energy expenditure (out):** the energy your body uses.

[Diagram: Energy balance seesaws: balanced, intake above expenditure, expenditure above intake]
When energy in equals energy out, body weight stays stable. If you regularly take in more than you use, the extra is stored (mostly as fat). If you use more than you take in, stored energy is used.

Growing teenagers need plenty of energy. Skipping meals or following fad diets is not healthy for young people.

### Where does energy go?
[Diagram: A stacked bar showing BMR, activity and digestion as shares of daily energy use]
- **Basal metabolic rate (BMR):** the energy needed to keep you alive at rest (breathing, circulation, repair). It is usually the **largest** share.
- **Physical activity:** the most variable part, so the part you can change most.
- **Digestion:** a small amount of energy is used to process food.

**Estimated energy requirement (EER) = BMR × physical activity level (PAL)**

Energy needs depend on **age, sex, body size and composition, growth, and activity level**. Active athletes need more.

### Reading food labels
| Label feature | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Serving size** | Check whether numbers are per 100 g or per serving |
| **Energy (kcal / kJ)** | The fuel a serving provides |
| **Sugars** | Lower is better for added sugar |
| **Saturated fat** | Lower is better |
| **Salt / sodium** | Lower is better |
| **Fibre** | Higher is better |
| **Ingredients list** | Listed from **largest to smallest** amount by weight |

Compare **per 100 g** columns to compare two products fairly.

## Lesson: Definition of health and well-being

## What you will learn
- Define **health** and **well-being**.
- Describe **physical, mental and social** well-being.
- Explain how exercise can improve each aspect.

### Definitions
The World Health Organization describes **health** as *a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity*.

**Well-being** is how well you are doing and feeling overall. It has three linked parts:

[Diagram: Health and well-being: physical, mental and social]
| Aspect | What it means | How exercise can help |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | How well your body works: heart, lungs, muscles, bones, energy, sleep | Stronger heart and muscles, healthy body composition, better sleep |
| **Mental (emotional)** | How you think and feel and how you cope with stress | Reduces stress and anxiety, boosts mood and confidence |
| **Social** | Your relationships and sense of belonging | Teamwork, friendships, feeling part of a group |

The three parts affect each other. For example, stress (mental) can cause poor sleep (physical) and make you avoid friends (social). Looking after one part helps the others.


## Lesson: Safe practice: the PAR-Q

## What you will learn
- Explain what the **PAR-Q** is and why it is used.
- Describe other safe-practice steps for exercise.

### The PAR-Q
The **Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q)** is a short set of **yes/no questions** used to screen people **before they start or increase exercise**. It asks about things such as chest pain, dizziness, bone or joint problems, heart conditions and medication.

[Diagram: How the PAR-Q is used]
- If **all answers are no**, it is generally safe to start gradually.
- If **any answer is yes**, speak to a doctor or parent/guardian before increasing activity.
- It is a **screening tool**, not a medical diagnosis, and only one part of safe practice.

### Other safe-practice steps
- **Warm up** and **cool down** properly.
- Check **equipment** and the **environment** (surface, space, weather).
- Wear suitable **clothing, footwear and protective equipment**.
- **Hydrate** and eat appropriately.
- Use correct **technique** and progress gradually.
- Follow the rules and listen to instructions.
- **Stop and tell someone** if you feel pain, dizzy or unwell.
- Tell your teacher or coach about any **injury, illness or medical condition**.


## Lesson: Treatment of injuries

## What you will learn
- Describe common sports injuries.
- Explain the **RICE** treatment of soft-tissue injuries and newer approaches.
- Know when to seek medical help.

### Common injuries
| Injury | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Sprain** | Damage to a **ligament** (bone to bone) |
| **Strain** | Damage to a **muscle or tendon** |
| **Bruise (contusion)** | Bleeding under the skin after impact |
| **Fracture** | A broken bone |
| **Dislocation** | Bones forced out of position at a joint |
| **Blister / graze** | Damage to the skin surface |

### RICE for soft-tissue injuries
[Diagram: RICE: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation]
| Letter | Action |
|---|---|
| **R**est | Stop and rest the injured part to prevent further damage |
| **I**ce | Apply a cold pack wrapped in a cloth for 10-20 minutes at a time (never directly on the skin) |
| **C**ompression | Support with a firm bandage, not too tight |
| **E**levation | Raise the injured part above heart level to reduce swelling |

Some guides add **P** for **Protect** (PRICE). Newer guidance for soft-tissue injuries is **PEACE** (first days: **P**rotect, **E**levate, **A**void anti-inflammatories, **C**ompress, **E**ducate) and **LOVE** (afterwards: **L**oad gently, **O**ptimism, **V**ascularisation, **E**xercise). Ask your teacher which approach your course uses.

### When to get medical help
Severe pain, obvious deformity, numbness or tingling, being unable to put weight on the injury, a head injury, or no improvement after a few days. **In an emergency, call for help and tell a teacher or adult at once.**

## Lesson: Motivation: definition and types

## What you will learn
- Define **motivation**.
- Compare **intrinsic** and **extrinsic** motivation with examples.
- Suggest ways to build motivation.

### Definition
**Motivation** is the **drive** that makes us start an activity, keep going when it is hard, and try our best.

[Diagram: Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation]
| Type | Where it comes from | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | From **within**: enjoyment, challenge, personal satisfaction | Playing because it is fun; wanting to master a skill |
| **Extrinsic** | From **outside**: rewards or pressure | Trophies, medals, prizes, praise, avoiding punishment |

Extrinsic rewards can be **tangible** (trophies, money) or **intangible** (praise, recognition). Both types can be useful. Intrinsic motivation tends to last longer. If rewards become the only reason to take part, enjoyment can fall when the rewards stop.

### Building motivation
- Set clear, achievable goals and track progress.
- Choose activities you enjoy and add variety.
- Train with friends or a team.
- Celebrate effort and improvement, not only winning.
- Remind yourself *why* you started.


## Lesson: Anxiety, arousal and performance

## What you will learn
- Define **arousal** and **anxiety**.
- Describe **drive theory** and the **inverted-U theory**.
- Explain how optimal arousal depends on the skill.

### Key terms
- **Arousal:** how alert and physically/mentally activated you are, from very calm to extremely excited.
- **Anxiety:** a negative emotional state of worry or nervousness, often about performing. It can be **cognitive** (worrying thoughts) or **somatic** (physical signs like a racing heart).
- **Performance:** how well you carry out a skill or task.

### Drive theory
Drive theory says that as **arousal (drive) increases, performance increases in a straight line**.

[Diagram: Drive theory: performance rises in a straight line as arousal increases]
It is most useful for **simple, well-learned skills** and experienced performers. Critics say it does not explain why performance often **drops** when athletes are over-excited.

### Inverted-U theory
The **inverted-U theory** says performance is best at a **moderate (optimal) level of arousal**. Too little arousal (under-aroused: bored, slow, unfocused) and too much (over-aroused: anxious, tense, poor decisions) both reduce performance. The graph is shaped like an upside-down "U".

[Diagram: The inverted-U graph: under-aroused, optimal arousal and over-aroused]
### The best level depends on the skill
[Diagram: Optimal arousal is lower for fine skills and higher for gross skills]
- **Fine skills** (small, precise movements: golf putt, archery, snooker) work best at **lower** arousal.
- **Gross skills** (large, powerful movements: rugby tackle, shot put, weightlifting) work best at **higher** arousal.
- Optimal arousal also varies with the **person** (experienced or beginner, personality) and the **situation**.


## Lesson: Measurement of anxiety and well-being

## What you will learn
- Explain how **questionnaires** are used to measure anxiety, arousal, confidence and well-being.
- Interpret before-and-after results.
- Describe the strengths and limits of self-report questionnaires.

### Why measure?
We cannot see anxiety directly, so we ask athletes to **rate how they feel**. A questionnaire gives a **baseline** (starting score) that can be compared after training, before a competition, or after trying a technique.

### Examples of questionnaires
| Questionnaire | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **SCAT** (Sport Competition Anxiety Test) | General tendency to feel anxious in competition (trait anxiety) |
| **CSAI-2** (Competitive State Anxiety Inventory-2) | Anxiety and confidence **right before** a competition: **cognitive anxiety, somatic anxiety and self-confidence** |
| **Well-being or mood scales** (for example the WHO-5 well-being index) | Overall mood, energy and well-being over recent weeks |

In these tools you rate statements on a scale, for example from 1 (*not at all*) to 4 (*very much so*):
- *"I am concerned about performing badly."* (cognitive anxiety)
- *"My heart is racing."* (somatic anxiety)
- *"I feel confident I can meet the challenge."* (self-confidence)

[Diagram: Example scores before and after psychological skills training]
### Strengths and limitations
- **Strengths:** quick, cheap, private, and easy to repeat.
- **Limitations:** answers are **self-reported** and can be inaccurate or untruthful; mood changes day to day; wording and scales may not suit everyone; they show scores, not the *cause*.
- They are **not a diagnosis**. If you are worried about your anxiety or mood, talk to a trusted adult, such as a teacher, school counsellor, parent or doctor.


## Lesson: Responses to arousal

## What you will learn
- Describe the **physiological** and **psychological** responses to arousal.
- Explain why moderate arousal helps but too much can harm performance.

### What happens when we become aroused?
When you are about to compete or face a challenge, the body's **sympathetic nervous system** (the "fight or flight" response) prepares you for action. **Adrenaline** is released.

[Diagram: Physiological and psychological responses to arousal]
### Physiological (body) responses
- **Heart rate increases** to deliver more oxygen to muscles.
- **Breathing becomes faster and deeper.**
- **Sweating** increases to cool the body.
- **Muscle tension** increases (muscles prepare to act).
- **Blood is diverted** to the muscles (so the stomach can feel "butterflies" and the mouth can feel dry).

### Psychological (mind) responses
- Increased **alertness and focus** (helpful at the right level).
- **Worry, negative thoughts** and self-doubt.
- **Difficulty concentrating**, or attention narrowing too far.
- **Irritability or mood changes.**
- Changes in **confidence**, which can rise or fall.

A moderate level helps you perform. Too much can cause **tight muscles, poor co-ordination and bad decisions**, which is why athletes learn techniques to control arousal.


## Lesson: Psychological skills training (PST)

## What you will learn
- Describe four psychological skills: **mental imagery, breathing techniques, muscle relaxation and self-talk**.
- Explain how each can control arousal and improve performance.

[Diagram: The PST toolkit: imagery, breathing, relaxation, self-talk]
**Psychological skills training (PST)** is regular practice of mental techniques, just as you practise physical skills.

### 1. Mental imagery
Picturing yourself performing a skill successfully, using **all senses** (what you see, hear and feel).
- **Internal imagery:** from your own point of view.
- **External imagery:** watching yourself like a video.
- Use it to rehearse skills, build confidence and prepare for a competition. Practise it regularly and keep it realistic and positive.

### 2. Breathing techniques
Slow, controlled breathing lowers heart rate and calms the body. In **box breathing** you breathe in for 4, hold for 4, breathe out for 4 and hold for 4.

[Diagram: Box breathing: in for 4, hold 4, out for 4, hold 4]
### 3. Muscle relaxation
**Progressive muscle relaxation** means **tensing** a muscle group for a few seconds, then **releasing** it, and working through the body (feet, legs, stomach, arms, shoulders, face). It reduces **somatic** (physical) anxiety and helps sleep.

### 4. Self-talk
What you say to yourself, silently or aloud.
| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| **Positive** | "I've trained for this." |
| **Instructional** | "Eyes on the ball." "Elbow up." |
| **Motivational** | "Keep pushing!" |
| **Negative (avoid)** | "I always miss." |

Change negative thoughts into helpful ones: "Don't mess up" becomes "Focus on a smooth release." Short **cue words** ("smooth", "explode") help you refocus.


## Lesson: Goal setting: SMART format

## What you will learn
- Write goals in **SMART** format.
- Explain why goals improve motivation and performance.
- Distinguish outcome, performance and process goals.

[Diagram: SMART goals]
| Letter | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S** | **Specific**: clear and detailed | Improve my 1500 m time |
| **M** | **Measurable**: you can track it with numbers | From 8:00 to 7:30 |
| **A** | **Achievable**: challenging but realistic | A 30-second improvement is realistic |
| **R** | **Relevant**: matters to you and your sport | Helps me make the school team |
| **T** | **Time-bound**: has a deadline | Within 8 weeks |

**Example SMART goal:** *"Improve my 1500 m time from 8:00 to 7:30 within 8 weeks by training three times a week."*

### Why set goals?
Goals give **direction**, increase **motivation and focus**, let you **measure progress**, and build **confidence** when you achieve them.

### Types of goal
| Type | Focus | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome** | The final result (depends on others) | Win the tournament |
| **Performance** | Your own standard | Run 1500 m in under 7:30 |
| **Process** | The actions needed to perform well | Keep my head up and arms relaxed when running |

Break a **long-term goal** into **short-term goals** (stepping stones), review progress often and adjust goals when needed.
