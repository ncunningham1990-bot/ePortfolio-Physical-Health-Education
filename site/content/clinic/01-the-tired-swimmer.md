Patient: Maya, 15, club swimmer
Intro: Maya trains six mornings a week and is aiming for regionals in 8 weeks. Her coach has asked you, the clinic coach, to take a look at her training diary.

Scene: Maya says she feels tired all the time and her swim times have stopped improving. What do you do first?
Data: Resting heart rate: 58 → 68 bpm over 2 weeks | Sleep: about 6 hours | Training: 6 sessions a week
+ Look at her diary and ask about sleep, mood, illness and how her training load has changed | Good start. A rising resting heart rate on its own does not tell you the cause, so you gather the full picture before deciding anything.
~ Ask her how she is feeling and leave it there | Her feelings matter, but you also have measurable data in her diary. Use both.
- Tell her to swim harder to push through | Think about what her resting heart rate trend and tiredness might be telling you before adding more work.
- Ignore it, because tiredness is normal for athletes | Some tiredness is normal, but a clear change over two weeks combined with stalled times deserves a closer look.

Scene: Her diary shows she added two sessions a fortnight ago and has had no rest day since. What is the most likely explanation?
Data: Load up about 30% | Rest days: 0 | Mood: irritable
+ Over-training: her training load has risen faster than her body can recover | Yes. A rising resting heart rate, tiredness, poor sleep and a drop in performance are classic indicators of over-training.
- She has become fitter | Fitter people normally see a lower resting heart rate, so look again at the direction of her numbers.
- She is just lacking motivation | Motivation may be affected, but it does not explain the physical signs in her data.
- It is a faulty heart rate monitor | A faulty monitor is possible, but several different signs point the same way.

Scene: What change to her programme would you recommend?
+ Reduce the load for a short time, add rest days and keep light, enjoyable sessions | A planned reduction lets her body recover and adapt, and light sessions keep her routine and fitness ticking over.
~ Stop training completely for two months | She does need recovery, but a complete break risks reversibility. Reduce the load instead of removing it.
- Keep the same load but add a longer warm-up | A longer warm-up does not solve the underlying imbalance between training and recovery.
- Add one more hard session to make up for the slow times | More load on a body that is not recovering is likely to make the problem worse.

Scene: Two weeks later her resting heart rate is back to 59 bpm. How should the coach plan the build-up to regionals?
+ Increase the load gradually, include rest days and keep monitoring resting heart rate and sleep | Progressive overload with planned recovery lets her improve without tipping into over-training again.
- Go straight back to the old schedule | Jumping back to what caused the problem risks the same result.
- Only train at the weekend | Too little training will not give her enough overload to reach her goal.
- Stop checking her data because she is fine now | Monitoring is how you spot trouble early, so it is most useful when training increases.

Debrief: You noticed that signs of over-training are a pattern, not a single number. The fix was a smaller training load with rest, then a gradual build-up and ongoing monitoring.
